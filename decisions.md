# Decisions

- Candidates = all 299 unique drugs from any of the six signatures in Table S3 (Oskotsky et al. 2024), not only the 221 shared by all six, so no candidate is missed.
- Naproxen, mefenamic acid and letrozole were on both lists; I kept them only as standard because they are already used as treatments, leaving 296 candidates.
- Dropped 15 candidates that had no RxNorm match, because drugs not in RxNorm almost never appear in FAERS, leaving 281 candidates and 15 standard drugs (296 total):
  - Lab research compounds never sold as medicines: camptothecin, nocodazole, staurosporine, geldanamycin, puromycin, 8-azaguanine, alsterpaullone, bicuculline, ginkgolide A.
  - Not approved in the US (used elsewhere or only in animals): hexestrol, pentolonium, sulfaphenazole, epirizole, ribostamycin, apramycin.
  - Meta --> results --> total: 25500
  - Ibuprofen side-effect counts (&count=patient.reaction.reactionmeddrapt.exact): top side effect is PAIN with 2,477 reports, then NAUSEA 2,046 and VOMITING 1,790.
  - Population: female patients, age 12–35 at onset, all report years. Reason: endometriosis symptoms usually begin in adolescence, and long-term treatment safety matters most for young patients.
- Drugs are matched on openFDA's patient.drug.openfda.generic_name, as planned, because it is consistent and matches the hand query (ibuprofen = 25,500 reports); this field only covers drugs with a current US label, so 98 drugs (mostly sold outside the US) get zero reports and count as insufficient data, which is a stated limitation.
- The estradiol search excludes reports with ethinyl estradiol, because searching "estradiol" also matches "ethinyl estradiol" (a different drug in most birth-control pills) and inflated estradiol from 2,266 to 40,576 reports.
- Headline number: 1,124,641 FAERS reports for female patients aged 12–35 (from scripts/fetch_counts.py, saved in data/clean/drug_totals.csv).
- Drugs with under 50 reports are labelled "not enough data", because with so few reports a side-effect signal can appear or vanish by chance; this applies to 142 candidates (98 with zero reports, 44 with 1–49) and no standard drugs, leaving 154 drugs with enough data (full list in data/clean/not_enough_data.csv).
- Priority side effects, chosen before seeing any drug results so they cannot be cherry-picked. These 12 exact MedDRA preferred terms matter for years of use by young women, and none is a symptom of endometriosis itself (counts are reports in the population of 1,124,641):
  - DEEP VEIN THROMBOSIS (6,983): blood clots are the best-known serious risk of hormonal treatments such as ethinyl estradiol, and a lifetime risk adds up over years of use.
  - PULMONARY EMBOLISM (10,195): a clot that travels to the lungs can be fatal, and it is the most dangerous result of the same clotting risk.
  - OSTEOPOROSIS (870): drugs that lower estrogen (like leuprolide and elagolix) thin the bones, and losing bone before age 30 means a lower peak bone mass for life.
  - BONE DENSITY DECREASED (645): it catches early bone loss that is reported before osteoporosis is diagnosed.
  - DEPRESSION (20,329): mood changes are a common reason young women stop hormonal treatments; depression is also more common with chronic pain, which is why it is compared against other drugs, not counted alone.
  - SUICIDAL IDEATION (10,995): it is the most serious form of mood side effect, and teenagers and young adults are the age group most at risk.
  - ANXIETY (27,994): it is a frequent mood side effect of hormonal drugs and affects daily life over years of treatment.
  - HEPATOTOXICITY (1,396): liver damage builds up with long-term use, and several candidate drugs are known to strain the liver.
  - DRUG-INDUCED LIVER INJURY (2,298): it is the other term reporters use for liver damage, so both terms are needed to catch it.
  - HYPERTENSION (6,677): high blood pressure from years of treatment raises the risk of stroke and heart disease later in life.
  - ABORTION SPONTANEOUS (14,223): many patients are of childbearing age or trying to conceive, so a drug linked to miscarriage matters greatly.
  - MATERNAL EXPOSURE DURING PREGNANCY (28,609): it flags drugs often taken during pregnancy, where safety for the baby matters. This replaces the suggested FOETAL EXPOSURE DURING PREGNANCY (only 414 reports), because FAERS uses the "foetal" term for reports about the baby and the "maternal" term for reports about the woman, who is the patient in this population.
- The Benjamini-Hochberg correction covers only drugs with enough data (65,063 drug-side effect pairs), because drugs with under 50 reports are not ranked, and including them would make the correction stricter for no benefit.
- Positive controls (well-known side effects that the method should find): all 4 are flagged as signals and stay significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction, so the method detects known side effects.
  - Ethinyl estradiol + DEEP VEIN THROMBOSIS: 4,733 reports, ROR 67.9 (95% CI 64.5–71.5), signal.
  - Ethinyl estradiol + PULMONARY EMBOLISM: 6,185 reports, ROR 52.0 (95% CI 49.9–54.2), signal.
  - Leuprolide + HOT FLUSH: 661 reports, ROR 80.3 (95% CI 73.3–88.0), signal.
  - Elagolix + HOT FLUSH: 89 reports, ROR 42.4 (95% CI 33.9–53.1), signal.
- Ranking order: drugs are sorted by priority signals after Benjamini-Hochberg correction first, then before correction, then by total reports (more first), because the corrected count is the stricter test and a clean result backed by more reports is better supported. 154 drugs are ranked (139 candidates, 15 standard); 25 have zero priority signals after correction.
- Top 15 review (first-pass notes by Claude; check prices and usage yourself before citing them). Question for each: is it already widely used by young women, and is it cheap and generic?
  - 1 Tobramycin (3,103 reports, 0 signals): antibiotic, generic; used long-term by young women only as an inhaled drug for cystic fibrosis (expensive) or briefly as cheap eye drops; it is not absorbed when swallowed, so it is not a practical endometriosis drug.
  - 2 Orlistat (2,690, 0): weight-loss drug sold cheaply over the counter (Alli) and used by many young women; it works inside the gut and is barely absorbed, which likely explains its clean profile.
  - 3 Colchicine (1,250, 0): cheap generic for gout and inflammatory conditions; used long-term by young women with familial Mediterranean fever or pericarditis, but overdose is dangerous because the safe dose range is narrow.
  - 4 Miconazole (817, 0): cheap generic antifungal, used very widely by young women as a vaginal or skin cream; applied locally, so little reaches the bloodstream.
  - 5 Acetazolamide (787, 0): cheap generic; already taken for years by young women with idiopathic intracranial hypertension (a condition mostly of young women), so it is the strongest fit for both questions.
  - 6 Azacitidine (432, 0): cancer chemotherapy, expensive and toxic; not suitable for long-term use, and its reports come from people with serious illness.
  - 7 Ivermectin (429, 0): cheap generic antiparasitic, usually taken as a short course or as a rosacea cream; well tolerated, but there is no experience with years of daily use.
  - 8 Beclometasone (311, 0): inhaled steroid for asthma, widely used by young women for years; generic in many countries but not cheap in the US; inhaled, so little reaches the rest of the body.
  - 9 Deferoxamine (247, 0): generic iron-removing drug, used for years by young people with thalassaemia, but it needs long infusions under the skin, so it is impractical.
  - 10 Irinotecan (230, 0): cancer chemotherapy, toxic; not suitable.
  - 11 Fulvestrant (218, 0): breast-cancer drug that blocks estrogen, which fits endometriosis biology; generic but expensive monthly injections, rarely used in young women; its estrogen-blocking could cause bone loss that 218 reports are too few to detect.
  - 12 Capsaicin (136, 0): cheap over-the-counter cream or patch for pain; applied to skin, so little reaches the bloodstream.
  - 13 Flecainide (101, 0): cheap generic heart-rhythm drug, used by some young women with fast heart rhythms; it can cause dangerous rhythms in people with heart disease, so it is not for broad use.
  - 14 Nafarelin (100, 0): a standard endometriosis treatment (GnRH nasal spray), brand-only and expensive; its zero signals contrast with leuprolide and elagolix (bone and mood signals), but 100 reports give little power to detect them.
  - 15 Dipyridamole (98, 0): cheap generic blood thinner, mostly used by older people after stroke; it may worsen heavy periods, which matters in endometriosis.
- Observations on the ranking:
  - Fewer reports mean less power to detect a signal, so small drugs rise to the top (median 311 reports in the top 15 vs 675 for the rest); zero signals is best supported for tobramycin, orlistat and colchicine (1,000+ reports each).
  - Large, widely used drugs have the opposite problem: ibuprofen (25,500 reports, rank 146) and ethinyl estradiol (38,310, rank 97) show many signals partly because enough reports exist to detect even small differences.
  - Several top drugs are local (creams, inhalers, gut-only, eye drops), so their clean profiles reflect low exposure in the body; they would also be unlikely to reach endometriosis lesions in those forms.
  - Cancer drugs (azacitidine, irinotecan, fulvestrant) are not realistic options for years of use, whatever their signal count.
  - Acetazolamide is the candidate that best meets all three tests: zero priority signals, cheap and generic, and already used long-term by young women.
